When Enforcement, Firearms Policy, and Spending Pull Apart

Many people hold several of these views at once. The offices that control them are not the same.

Common overlapping priorities

  • Stronger immigration enforcement and faster removal of people who are in the country unlawfully.
  • Protection of existing firearms rights, including recent reductions in federal restrictions on certain accessories and configurations.
  • Limits on overall federal spending and opposition to large new entitlement or industrial-subsidy programs.
  • Support for legal, skills- or family-based immigration pathways that do not undermine enforcement.

These positions frequently conflict across party lines and across levels of government. A straight-ticket vote requires sacrificing at least one of them.

Which offices actually matter

Immigration enforcement intensity is shaped by the President (executive guidance and agency priorities), Congress (appropriations and statutory changes), state governors and attorneys general (cooperation agreements and state-level enforcement), and local sheriffs (jail cooperation).

Federal firearms rules (NFA items, agency guidance, appropriations riders) are controlled mainly by Congress and the executive branch. State legislatures and governors set state-level carry, preemption, and accessory rules.

Spending and industrial policy are primarily congressional and presidential. State-level equivalents involve governors and legislatures.

Because the levers sit in different places, it is possible to advance more than one priority by choosing different candidates in different races.

Applying the method

  1. Rank the priorities above (or your own version of them) in order.
  2. For each high-ranked item, note the one or two offices with the most direct control.
  3. In those specific races, compare the candidates on the relevant power rather than on party label.
  4. Accept a split ticket if that is what the mapping produces.

The same process works if your ordering is different. The point is to match votes to offices instead of matching votes to a single party.
