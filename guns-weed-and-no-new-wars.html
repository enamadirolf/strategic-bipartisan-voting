Guns, Weed, and No New Wars

Three priorities. Different offices. A straight ticket usually drops at least one.

The overlap

  • Opposition to new U.S. military interventions and to open-ended authorizations for the use of force.
  • Protection of existing firearms rights, including resistance to new federal restrictions on common configurations and accessories.
  • Support for removing cannabis from the most restrictive federal schedules and for leaving state-level legalization intact.

These views are frequently held together. The officials who can advance or block each one are not the same, and the major parties split them differently.

Who actually controls each piece

New wars and force authorizations are primarily congressional (AUMFs, funding, war-powers resolutions) and presidential (initiating or expanding operations). Individual senators and representatives have direct votes; the executive sets day-to-day policy.

Federal firearms rules are set by Congress (statutes and appropriations riders) and by executive-branch agencies operating under those statutes. State legislatures and governors control state-level carry, preemption, and accessory laws.

Cannabis scheduling and enforcement are federal (Congress and the executive, including rescheduling or appropriations limits on enforcement). State legalization and regulation are controlled by state legislatures and governors. Local prosecutors and sheriffs affect day-to-day enforcement intensity.

Because the levers are split, a voter can advance more than one of these priorities by treating each race as a separate choice.

When the Libertarian candidate cannot win

In most races a Libertarian nominee has no realistic path to victory. Voting for that candidate still sends a signal, but it does not change who holds the office or how the office is used.

The mapping strategy offers a different route to the same priorities. Identify the major-party (or viable independent) candidate in each race who is closest to your ranking on the specific power that office holds. A senator who will vote against new AUMFs, a representative who will oppose new federal gun restrictions, or a state legislator who will protect existing cannabis rules can move the outcome even if no Libertarian is elected. Over multiple offices and cycles, those incremental shifts are how constrained priorities advance inside a two-party system.

The choice is not between “pure” and “compromised.” It is between casting a vote that cannot affect the office and casting a vote that can move the office closer to one of your ranked priorities.

Applying the method

  1. Rank the three priorities (or your own ordering of them).
  2. For each high-ranked item, identify the one or two offices with the most direct control in your location.
  3. In those races, compare the viable candidates on the relevant power and record rather than on party label or on the presence of a long-shot option.
  4. Split the ticket if the mapping says that is the higher-impact choice.

The same steps work if your ranking is different or if you add or drop one of the three. The point is to match votes to offices that can actually change the outcome.
